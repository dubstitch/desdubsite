The thinking behind the work

Everything
comes with me.

I'm a front-end developer and web content strategist. But the way I work has never fit neatly inside one job title, and I don't think it should.

01 / The through line

I don't start from zero every time I learn something new.

Every skill I've developed changes how I approach the next thing. I began my career in development, learning to understand how systems behave, how interfaces are built and what happens behind the experience someone sees. Content strategy added another perspective. Instead of asking only whether something could be built, I started asking whether it made sense, whether someone could find it, understand it, trust it and use it.

Those ways of thinking don't compete. They make each other stronger. I can evaluate a digital experience as a developer, as a strategist, as a person responsible for maintaining the content and, importantly, as somebody who has to use the thing.

Nothing I've learned gets left behind just because my title changes.
02 / The philosophy

Everything connects.

I believe the most interesting solutions come from bringing different kinds of knowledge into the same room. A CMS issue can be a usability issue. A layout problem can reveal a content problem. A technical workaround can make a publishing workflow easier for an entire team. A seemingly small detail can change whether somebody feels confident enough to take the next step.

I use every tool that belongs in the solution. Sometimes that's HTML, CSS or JavaScript. Sometimes it's information architecture, documentation, analytics, a wireframe or a conversation with the people who know the audience best. I'm not interested in defending artificial boundaries between disciplines when crossing them can make the experience better.

03 / The human part

The person doing the work matters.

I've never experienced creativity as something confined to work hours or a particular software application. Making things, experimenting, noticing patterns and finding better ways to use what I have are parts of how I move through the world. I crochet, create, care for plants and animals, and make room for interests that don't need a professional justification.

I also build and support websites for friends and family, from small business storefronts in Square and Shopify to everyday content updates and visual work in Photoshop. Working with different tools outside an enterprise environment keeps me close to the realities of people who have to manage their own sites. It makes me more resourceful in my day job, too.

Those parts of my life aren't distractions from my work. They inform my patience, my curiosity, my eye for detail and my willingness to try another approach. Working with my hands reminds me that structure and feeling can coexist. Caring for living things reminds me to pay attention. Creativity reminds me there's rarely only one useful way to make something.

Making and experimenting

Crochet, design and creating things from an idea strengthen how I think through structure, iteration and execution.

Noticing and caring

My curiosity about people, animals and the world around me makes me pay closer attention to real behavior, not just assumptions.

04 / The journey

I found my direction by refusing to lose the rest of myself.

My career hasn't been a perfectly straight line, and I no longer need it to be. Development gave me a foundation. Working with different CMS platforms taught me how to adapt. Content strategy challenged me to think beyond implementation. My creative interests kept me open to possibilities that didn't always have an obvious name.

I've learned that growing professionally doesn't require shrinking everything else that makes me who I am. It requires trusting the connections I've built, continuing to learn and holding on to the parts of myself that give the work meaning.

I want to make things that work. I also want them to feel like somebody cared.
05 / What that means in practice

Thoughtful work is both technical and human.

I try to understand the real problem before I choose a solution. I look at how content fits into the system, how users move through it, what the people maintaining it will need and how the experience will hold up after launch. Then I bring together the tools, skills and collaborators that make the answer possible.

I care about accessibility because people deserve access. I care about clear content because attention is valuable. I care about clean implementation because someone else will inherit it. I care about beauty because usefulness and delight are not opposites.

That is what connects the work I've done, the things I'm still learning and the things I make because I love making them. It isn't a detour from my career. It's how I got here.

Where the perspective came from

A developer who understands the person using it.

Early in my career, I had the kind of guidance that stays with you. My mentor, Jeff Reightler, was there through my time at KRM and APEI. He's meticulous, and he pushed me to be thoughtful about the details. At APEI, working under UX director Evan also changed the way I saw development. I was in my twenties, learning that knowing how to build something wasn't the same as understanding how someone would experience it.

On a team of developers, I became the person other departments came to. I liked that part. I could understand what they wanted, translate it for the technical team and work out how to make it real. Later, I was building components and training people to create and manage their own pages. That taught me to think about the editor just as much as the visitor.

I still carry those lessons. I work independently now, but I can hear the questions those mentors would ask when I review my own work. And I've developed a perspective of my own along the way.

What pushes me

I don't see a limitation and automatically accept it as the answer.

I've worked in systems where a component almost does what you need, the CMS strips out certain markup, the rendered HTML has no useful IDs, or there isn't time for an external development team to rebuild something. That's when I get curious. What survives the sanitizer? What selector is reliable? Can I build the interaction around the constraints without creating a maintenance nightmare?

Some limitations are technical. Others come from processes, priorities and how an organization is set up to work. Experiencing both has pushed me to explore my own ideas here, where I can make the design decisions, write the code and find out what happens when I bring all of those skills together.

My favorite part of this field is making an idea happen, then seeing that it works for the people who need it. Good experiences are built from the whole picture, not just the code or the content.
